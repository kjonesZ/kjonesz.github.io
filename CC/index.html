Communication
in Context.

Helping students understand technology through the people, disciplines, and real problems around it.

Technology crosses disciplinary boundaries. Learning should help students make those connections—and communicate with the people who see the same problem from a different perspective.

A working definition

Communication in Context brings people from different disciplines into conversation around a shared problem, so students can connect technical knowledge with the setting in which it is used.

02 / Where it began

Experience gives knowledge a setting.

Faculty externships helped shape this idea: understanding a workplace means seeing more than its technology. It also means seeing how people work together, what decisions they face, and why a technical task matters.

That experience raises a teaching question: how can students encounter more of that context while they are learning? A shared problem can give different disciplines a reason to meet, explain their thinking, and learn from one another.

This outline develops the connection between faculty externships, learning across disciplines, and new program ideas.

03 / Guiding principles

Four ideas to build on.

01

Start with a shared problem.

Give the learning a purpose that students can understand before choosing a tool or solution.

02

Bring different perspectives.

Invite disciplines to explain what they notice, what they need, and what others might overlook.

03

Make the context visible.

Connect technical choices to people, workflows, constraints, and consequences.

04

Use conversation to connect.

Ask students to explain their reasoning, clarify unfamiliar language, and check for shared understanding.

04 / In practice

One problem. Several ways of seeing it.

Illustrative teaching scenario

A clinic wants to introduce a digital check-in system.

What would make the system useful, understandable, and appropriate for the people who rely on it?

  • TechnologyHow will the system connect, work reliably, and support its users?
  • HealthcareHow will it fit patient needs and the daily flow of care?
  • BusinessWhat resources, training, and process changes will it require?
  • Communication & designWill patients understand what to do, including when they need help?
  • CybersecurityWhat information is needed, who should access it, and how will it be protected?

Students compare these perspectives, identify tradeoffs, and explain a proposed approach together.

An example for developing the concept; not a description of an existing program.

05 / From idea to learning

Begin with a small, shared experience.

A first experiment could bring two or more disciplines together around one challenge. The purpose is to explore what students learn when they have to connect their expertise with someone else’s.

Shared problem→Different perspectives→Shared understanding
  1. Frame the situation. Describe the people, setting, and problem without prescribing the answer.
  2. Surface the questions. Let each discipline identify what it needs to understand.
  3. Develop an approach. Ask students to negotiate priorities and explain their choices.
  4. Reflect on the connections. Identify how another perspective changed the proposed solution.
  5. Use what emerges. Consider what the experience suggests for future assignments, partnerships, or program development.

06 / Questions to develop

Keep the concept open.

These questions can guide the next version of the framework.

What does “context” need to include?

People, purpose, workplace practices, disciplinary language, constraints, and consequences are possible starting points. Which are essential to the idea?

What should students be able to do differently?

Possible outcomes include explaining a technical idea to another discipline, asking better questions, recognizing tradeoffs, and adapting a solution to its setting.

How will we recognize meaningful learning?

Look for evidence that students can explain how another perspective changed their thinking. Develop criteria that value both disciplinary knowledge and the connections between disciplines.

How can faculty externships inform the experience?

Explore how faculty can turn workplace observations into shared problems, teaching examples, and conversations with colleagues in other disciplines.

What is the first experiment worth trying?

Choose a manageable problem, a small group of collaborators, and one clear learning goal. Use the results to refine the concept before expanding it.
